The story

He wanted the limit, not the sum

Padishah Murr ad-Din put no faith in the calligraphy of his scribes: paper endures any lie, provided the lie is tidy. He wanted something a paw could rest on.

The seals were broken. Eleven years of disorder spilled out of the dark — ruby pressed under the dull weight of onyx, pearl rubbed raw among spinel, and on the sapphires a hollow left by whoever had slept there while the realm was called rich. Drawing the border of all that splendour falls to you.

The rules

The matter sets them, not the designer

  • Sacks are cut to the count. As many places as there are stones of that kind — no slack, no crowding.
  • Like to like, then seal. A full sack pulls its own drawstring.
  • Two sets of rules. Strict: the seal is final, and one careless move can cost the audit. Forgiving: a tap tears the seal off again. Strict is the default.
  • The chest cannot be sealed. It holds what has no place yet, and by the last knot it must stand empty.
  • Nothing finishes itself. The last stone goes in by your hand — no auto-complete, ever.

How it thinks

No unwinnable board can exist

Deals are unwound, not guessed

A level is made by taking a sealed treasury apart with reversible moves. Every step is invertible by construction, so a way back always exists. The generator never needs a solver to vet its own work.

Beatable at the start, either way

Under strict rules the way back must avoid seals too, so the generator refuses any reverse move that would leave a sack full and uniform. Both sets of rules get a deal that can be finished — only your own hand can ruin it.

Solve bisects your history

A position with no way out stays that way after any move. So the history splits cleanly in two, and the last salvageable point is found by bisection — six probes on a fifty-move game instead of fifty.

Rewind, then carry on

In a dead end the game does not restart the board. It winds the stones back to the fork where your move was still sound, and plays from there — coldly, and without error.

Sound out of nothing

The stone's knock and the seal's click are synthesised at runtime through the Web Audio API. Not one audio file ships with the app.

Between audits

The padishah watches, and now and then speaks

Every tenth audit closes with a remark from the throne — a hundred of them, enough for a thousand chests. “Your predecessor kept records. The records burned. So did he.”

Every thirtieth, a painting fills the screen for five seconds: the vizier at his maps, the scales, the seal and the pigeon, the treasury clockwork.

Reach

Fourteen languages, three of them right to left

Interface and story in English, Russian, German, French, Spanish, Swedish, Finnish, Ukrainian, Arabic, Hebrew, Urdu, Kazakh, Kyrgyz and Chinese — the page turns itself around for Arabic, Hebrew and Urdu. The story is not a translation: it is the same tale told fourteen times.

The padishah's hundred remarks exist in English and Russian; the other languages hear him in English for now.

Permissions

None. Not one.

No internet, no location, no storage, no accounts, no ads, no analytics. Progress — the audit number, chests sealed, moves in all — never leaves the device, and the board itself is not even saved.

How it's made

Hand-authored bytecode, no build system

This is not a Gradle project and there is no Java source. The shell is smali — Dalvik bytecode written by hand — assembled with apktool; the game itself is one self-contained HTML file inside assets/.

# glue the game together, build, repack, sign
sh src/build.sh
java -jar apktool.jar b app -o hazna-unsigned.apk

# Android 11+ refuses to install unless resources.arsc is stored uncompressed
zip -X -0 hazna.apk resources.arsc
zip -X -9 -r hazna.apk AndroidManifest.xml classes.dex res assets

java -jar uber-apk-signer.jar -a hazna.apk --ks shumtugle.keystore

Two things that bite: the uncompressed resources.arsc above, and setDomStorageEnabled(true) in the WebView — without it the progress silently fails to save.
